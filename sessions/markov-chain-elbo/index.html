    From one hidden variable to a chain¶

    Sessions. This page continues From Jensen's inequality to the ELBO. It asks what goes wrong with a single hidden variable, replaces it with a chain of them, introduces the one assumption that keeps the chain manageable, and then puts the ELBO for one hidden variable next to the ELBO for the chain.

    0. Where we are¶

    The previous page ended with three formulas. The goal is maximum likelihood: make \(\E_{\text{data}}[\log p_\theta(x)]\) large. With a hidden variable \(z\) and any proxy \(q(z)\) for its posterior, the likelihood splits into a part we can compute and a gap:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \log p_\theta(x) &= \underbrace{\E_q\!\left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)} \right]}_{\text{ELBO}} + \KL\big( q(z) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x) \big) \\[4pt] &\ge \text{ELBO}. \end{aligned} \]

    Writing \(p_\theta(x, z) = p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z)\), the ELBO has two terms, and the training loss is its negative, averaged over the data:

    \[ \text{ELBO} = \underbrace{\E_q\big[ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{\KL\big( q(z) \,\|\, p(z) \big)}_{\text{stay near the prior}} . \]

    The rest of this page reuses these formulas, with \(z\) replaced by a chain of hidden variables.

    1. One hidden variable means one jump¶

    With one hidden variable, generating is a single jump: draw \(z\) from \(\N(0, I)\), pass it through the decoder once, and the sample is done. The decoder's output is a Gaussian, \(p_\theta(x \mid z) = \N\big(x;\ \mu_\theta(z),\ \sigma^2 I\big)\): one guess \(\mu_\theta(z)\), with a little noise around it.

    The problem shows up when we ask what the decoder should output for a given \(z\). If many different data points are plausible for that \(z\), the right answer is a distribution spread over all of them, possibly in several separate places. A Gaussian has one centre. The best single centre is the average of the plausible points, and the average of several data points is usually not a data point at all. For images, the average of several plausible images is a blur.

    The clip shows this on the spiral, first for one big jump and then for one small step.

    Training pairs each data point with a noisy version of itself. After a lot of noise, points from all over the spiral land in the same place, so a single noisy point could have come from almost anywhere. The best single Gaussian has to stretch over the whole spiral, and its samples fall between the arms. After a little noise, each point stays near where it was, the possible sources form one small patch, and a small Gaussian fits them.

    So a big jump asks a Gaussian to describe something that is not Gaussian, while a small step does not. The same problem hits the proxy \(q(z)\): the true posterior after one big jump is complicated, so a Gaussian proxy leaves a large gap in the bound.

    This suggests replacing the one big jump with many small ones.

    2. A chain of hidden variables¶

    Replace the single \(z\) by a sequence of hidden variables. We name them so that the data is at one end and the noise at the other:

    \[ x_T \;\to\; x_{T-1} \;\to\; \cdots \;\to\; x_1 \;\to\; x_0 . \]

    \(x_T\) is pure noise, \(x_0\) is the data, and generating walks from left to right, one small step at a time. We write \(x_{1:T}\) for all the hidden variables \((x_1, \ldots, x_T)\), and \(x_{0:T}\) for everything.

    How do we write down the joint distribution? The chain rule of note 2 works for any number of variables: produce them one at a time, each given everything produced so far.

    \[ \begin{aligned} p(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T)\; & p(x_{T-1} \mid x_T)\; p(x_{T-2} \mid x_{T-1}, x_T) \\ & \cdots\; p(x_0 \mid x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T). \end{aligned} \]

    This is always true, and it is a problem. Each step may depend on all the steps before it. The last factor takes \(T\) inputs, and with \(T = 1000\) that is a thousand images. A model of this form is huge, and the ELBO for it has no structure to exploit. The same goes for the proxy \(q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\).

    3. The Markov assumption¶

    3.1 What it says¶

    In words, the assumption is that the next state depends only on the current state, not on the path that led to it.

    For example, in a board game, where your piece goes next depends on the square it is on now and on the die. It does not depend on which squares it visited earlier. Knowing the whole history tells you nothing more than knowing the current square.

    An example in the chain. Take a slightly noisy image and add a little more noise. The result depends on the image you have now. How that image was made, from which clean picture and through which earlier noise, does not matter any more.

    Definition (Markov chain)

    A sequence of random variables is a Markov chain if each one, given the one just before it, does not depend on any earlier ones:

    \[ p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_{t+1}, \ldots, x_T) = p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t). \]

    Put this into the chain rule of section 2 and every factor loses all its inputs but one:

    \[ p(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t). \]

    So the model is fully described by one starting distribution and \(T\) one-step transitions.

    Without the assumption, every variable may depend on all the ones before it, and the number of arrows grows with the square of the chain's length. The Markov assumption keeps only the arrows between neighbours: each step looks at one state, the current one.

    Is the Markov assumption a claim about the data?

    No. It is a choice about how the model and the proxy are built. For the noising chain of the next section it is exactly true by construction: each step adds fresh noise to the current state only. For the generating chain it is a design decision, the same kind as choosing a Gaussian decoder, and the training objective tells us how well it works.

    3.2 The two chains of DDPM¶

    DDPM uses two Markov chains, one in each direction.

    The proxy: a fixed noising chain. In the previous page, the proxy \(q\) was ours to choose and learn. DDPM fixes it in advance to the simplest chain that destroys information gradually: shrink a little, add a little Gaussian noise, repeat. With numbers \(\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_T\) between \(0\) and \(1\), the noise schedule,

    \[ \begin{aligned} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) &= \N\!\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\; x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big), \\[4pt] q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) &= \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}). \end{aligned} \]

    It has no weights. Shrinking by \(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\) while adding noise of variance \(\beta_t\) keeps the overall size of the cloud steady, and after enough steps the chain arrives at \(\N(0, I)\) whatever the data was.

    The noising chain, step by step. Each step shrinks the current points a little and adds fresh Gaussian noise; the spiral dissolves into a standard Gaussian.

    The model: a learned denoising chain. It runs the other way, from noise to data, with one Gaussian step at a time:

    \[ \begin{aligned} p(x_T) &= \N(0, I), \\[4pt] p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) &= \N\!\big(x_{t-1};\ \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\ \sigma_t^2 I\big), \\[4pt] p_\theta(x_{0:T}) &= p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t). \end{aligned} \]

    One network \(\mu_\theta\) serves every step; it is told which step it is on. A Gaussian is a reasonable choice for each step because, as section 1 showed, each step is small.

    4. The ELBO, for one hidden variable and for a chain¶

    The bound of section 0 holds for any hidden variable and any proxy. So we do not need to prove anything new: we replace \(z\) by the whole chain \(x_{1:T}\), and \(q(z)\) by the noising chain.

    one hidden variable a chain of hidden variables
    hidden variable \(z\) \(x_{1:T}\)
    model \(p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z)\) \(p(x_T) \prod_t p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\)
    proxy \(q(z)\), learned \(\prod_t q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\), fixed
    the bound \(\log p_\theta(x) \ge \E_q\!\left[\log \dfrac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]\) \(\log p_\theta(x_0) \ge \E_q\!\left[\log \dfrac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\right]\)

    The single-variable ELBO on the left, and the same bound with \(z\) replaced by the whole chain on the right. The reconstruction term and the prior term each have a counterpart, and the chain adds one new term for every small step.

    4.1 Put in the two products¶

    Both the model and the proxy are Markov chains, so the log of each is a sum, and pairing terms with the same \(t\) gives

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{ELBO} = \E_q\Big[ & \log p(x_T) \\ & + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})} \Big]. \end{aligned} \]

    Each term is a ratio of \(p\) and \(q\). Is it a KL divergence? Not yet. The top is a distribution over \(x_{t-1}\), the bottom is a distribution over \(x_t\): they describe different variables, pointing in opposite directions along the chain. A KL compares two distributions over the same variable. To get one, the noising step on the bottom has to be turned around.

    4.2 Turn the noising step around¶

    Can we just reverse \(q\)? By Bayes' rule, \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1}) / q(x_t)\), and \(q(x_{t-1})\), \(q(x_t)\) are distributions of noisy data: they contain the unknown data distribution. So no.

    What if we also know where the chain started? During training we do: \(x_0\) is the training example. Two facts.

    • By the Markov property, adding \(x_0\) to the condition of a forward step changes nothing: \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0)\).
    • The chain rule, with \(x_0\) carried along, can produce the pair \((x_{t-1}, x_t)\) in either order: \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)\, q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = q(x_t \mid x_0)\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\).

    Together, for \(t \ge 2\):

    The rewrite

    \[ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \frac{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\; q(x_t \mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)} . \]

    The first factor is what we wanted: a backward step, a distribution over \(x_{t-1}\). In words, "given the noisy point and the clean example it came from, where was the chain one step earlier?" The other two factors cancel in the next step.

    Substituting, and the telescoping sum

    Split off the \(t = 1\) term and put the rewrite into the others. The extra factors form the sum

    \[ \sum_{t=2}^{T} \log \frac{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)}{q(x_t \mid x_0)} = \log \frac{q(x_1 \mid x_0)}{q(x_T \mid x_0)}, \]

    because the bottom of each fraction is the top of the next (write out \(T = 3\) to see it). The \(q(x_1 \mid x_0)\) left over cancels against the \(t = 1\) term, and what remains is

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{ELBO} = \E_q\Big[ & \log \frac{p(x_T)}{q(x_T \mid x_0)} + \sum_{t=2}^{T} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)} \\ & + \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) \Big]. \end{aligned} \]

    Now every ratio compares two distributions over the same variable, and averaging each over the distribution on its bottom gives minus a KL.

    4.3 The result, next to the one-variable ELBO¶

    Result (the DDPM objective)

    For one data point \(x_0\), \(\log p_\theta(x_0) \ge \text{ELBO} = -L_T - \sum_{t=2}^{T} L_{t-1} - L_0\), with

    \[ \begin{aligned} L_T &= \KL\big( q(x_T \mid x_0) \,\|\, p(x_T) \big), \\[4pt] L_{t-1} &= \E_{q(x_t \mid x_0)}\Big[ \KL\big( q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \\ &\hspace{6em} \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) \big) \Big], \\[4pt] L_0 &= -\,\E_{q(x_1 \mid x_0)}\big[ \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) \big]. \end{aligned} \]
    one hidden variable a chain what it asks
    reconstruction \(\E_q[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)]\) \(-L_0\) from the least noisy point, recover the data
    \(-\KL(q(z) \,\|\, p(z))\) \(-L_T\) after all the noising, be where the prior is
    none \(-L_{t-1}\), one for each \(t = 2, \ldots, T\) undo one small noising step correctly

    A few points about this result.

    • \(L_T\) has no \(\theta\) in it. The noising chain and the prior are both fixed, and the chain ends at \(\N(0, I)\) regardless of the data, so this term needs no training. In a VAE, the corresponding term has to be learned.
    • The new terms are where the learning happens. Each \(L_{t-1}\) compares the model's backward step with the true backward step of the noising chain, given the clean example. Section 1 promised that a small step is nearly Gaussian; here the target \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\) turns out to be exactly Gaussian, which the next page shows.
    • Every term involves one step. The objective is a sum of separate one-step problems, so training can pick one step at random for each example instead of running the whole chain.

    5. What comes next¶

    Two Gaussian facts finish the job: the noisy point \(x_t\) can be produced from \(x_0\) in one jump, and the backward target \(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\) is a Gaussian with an explicit mean. Then each \(L_{t-1}\) becomes a squared distance between two means (the same-variance KL of the previous page), and the whole objective becomes "predict the noise".
