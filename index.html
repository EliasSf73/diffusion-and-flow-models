    Diffusion and Flow Models, from Scratch¶

    About these notes¶

    I am writing these notes from the material I compiled while trying to understand diffusion and flow models: papers, textbooks, blog posts, lecture videos, and many conversations with large language models. They are first of all for me, as a self-contained reference I can come back to, and they are written so that anyone else can follow along.

    A few things I try to do along the way.

    • I try not to introduce a new term or piece of jargon unless it was introduced earlier in the series, is basic enough to assume, or is so widely used that you need to know it. When a term first appears, I try to explain it in plain words.
    • I try to include the questions I had while learning this material, together with the answers I eventually found, whether in a book, a paper, a website, or a conversation with an LLM. Many of them are the questions a careful reader will have too, and they are the parts I most want to be able to come back to.
    • I use LLMs to help turn my compiled notes into this series: to draft, to check derivations, to make figures, and to keep the writing consistent. The choices about what to include, in what order, and at what level are mine, and so are any mistakes.

    Elias Firisa

    Assumed background: a first course in probability (random variables, densities, expectation, Bayes' rule) and multivariable calculus. Measure theory is never used in the main text. Where a fully rigorous argument would need it, the rigor ledger at the end of the note says so and gives a reference, so nothing is hidden and nothing is required.

    How the notes are organised¶

    Every topic follows the same five-step pattern:

    \[ \boxed{\text{Intuition} \;\to\; \text{Math} \;\to\; \text{Derivation} \;\to\; \text{Code} \;\to\; \text{Connection forward}} \]
    • Intuition says what the object is for, with a picture where possible.
    • Math states the definitions and the theorem precisely.
    • Derivation proves what can be proved at our level. Anything we skip is recorded in the rigor ledger at the bottom of the page, with its assumptions and a reference.
    • Code runs the idea on one fixed toy dataset in two dimensions, so the same example is trained as a VAE, a DDPM, a score model, an SDE, a probability-flow ODE and a flow-matching model as the series progresses.
    • Connection forward says which later topic needs this one and why.

    Start with what is a generative model?, a short page with no equations that sets up the picture every later note relies on. The syllabus lists all forty-five topics with their status.

    The spine¶

    Most of the series exists to make one chain of equivalences concrete:

    \[ \text{DDPM} \;\to\; \text{score matching} \;\to\; \text{SDE} \;\to\; \text{probability-flow ODE} \;\to\; \text{flow matching}. \]

    Parts I to III build the language (probability, information, variational inference). Part IV derives DDPM in discrete time. Parts V and VI reinterpret it as score estimation and then as a stochastic differential equation. Part VII arrives at flow matching from the other direction and shows the two meet. Part VIII closes with conditioning and guidance, read as a control problem.

    Conventions¶

    • Vectors are column vectors in \(\R^d\). Data is \(x_0\), noised data is \(x_t\), latent variables are \(z\).
    • \(\N(x; \mu, \Sigma)\) is the Gaussian density evaluated at \(x\). \(\E_{p}[\cdot]\) is expectation under \(p\).
    • \(\KL(p \,\|\, q)\) is the Kullback–Leibler divergence.
    • The score of a density is \(\nabla_x \log p(x)\).
    • Time runs forward from clean data at \(t=0\) to noise at \(t=T\) (or \(t=1\)), in every part, including the flow-matching part, where much of the literature runs it the other way. We flag every place this matters.
